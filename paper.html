Can Non-Cooperative Game Theory Assist Modeling Security Implementation for Back-End Web App Development?

T. Koetting
Department of Electrical Engineering & Computer Science, University of Kansas
E-mail: tkoettin@stumail.jccc.edu

Abstract — In active development environments of web-based applications, back-end engineers and developers are constantly doing battle with bad actors probing for exploits. We explore the potential of non-cooperative game theory as a framework for enhancing back-end web development security and resource management. Most traditional strategies implemented by companies focus on static defense and reactive methods. By adopting a perspective influenced by game theory, we delve into the back and forth nature of the development timeline. We assume that the two exclusive contributors to the game in this framework are the development team and the bad actors. We finally explore whether or not the model being implemented should have characteristics aligned with flexibility or rigidity, and what influence their implementation environment has on the model’s final structure.

Key words — Game Theory • Web-App Development • Development Models • Nash Equilibrium • Stackelberg Defense

1. Introduction

Game theory has found myriad uses in security and infrastructure planning within organizations. Many aspects of non-cooperative game theory have been explored in network and IT with the two prominent model categories being security attack-defense analysis and security measurement [1]. For most purposes within security, most of these models are decidedly competitive and therefore non-cooperative due to the fact that both attackers and defenders are working to maximize their own payoff at the expense of their adversary [13].

There is also the given assumption depending upon the model used of known information, with the most realistic and computationally expensive models being Bayesian Games, which fall under the category of games of incomplete information, but Bayesian games also allow for knowledge of the payoffs of the adversary on both sides of the game, a situation which parallels active security efforts and offensive plans by the defending networks and attackers alike [14]. Through an evaluation function, the central defender can make decisions about which nodes and plans of action to take in terms of defensive strategy.

Within a network architecture, there is a need for multiple endpoint defense with moving attacks targeting different network nodes requiring distributed responses and strategies that are capable of choosing the most formidable defense while expending the least resources. These scenarios are also highly applicable to the exploitation prevention measures taken by defending web application developers; however, security research has sparsely touched game theory’s applications to software-level environments [2], with the bulk of the research focusing on active threats within IT infrastructure and the active reactions taken by the defending player and by the attacker [3].

The planning of infrastructure can be shown to have applicability to development, in the sense that given a significant element of software where exploits or vulnerabilities may exist given an inability to completely close off offensive opportunities due to budget constraints, where, for example given Rao et al. [3] and reinforced lines capable of being disconnected by $y_c$ cyber-attacks, we get the resulting equation:

$$p_{P|R} = \frac{f_P}{1 + N_L [y_c - x_c]^{+}}$$
(1)

where $N_L$ is the number of vulnerable vectors/lines, and where $x_c$ are the respective reinforcement hours of the component being attacked. So for components given the model derived from [3], we can understand the components as web application elements, where $y_c$ could stand for a non-specific attack on a particular deployment of Laravel or Ruby on Rails [15], and where $x_c$ would be the respective development hours spent on security and fortification implementations.

2. Mathematical Formulation & Diminishing Returns

Extensive work has also been done in the field dealing with software business competition, licensing dynamics [4], and development expenditures in relation to business decisions, with its main focus trending towards management science rather than security [5], [7]. The most applicable model within the management frameworks is that proposed by Hui & Tam [4] on game theoretical models applying to software functionality considerations in monopoly and duopoly markets, where higher software functionality has decreasing return and greater strain depending on the development team size and budget [18].

When given the software functionality $Q$ and its second-order derivative $Q''$:

$$Q'' = \frac{2}{5M} \left( (V + M) + \sqrt{(V + M)^2 - 5VM} \right)$$
(2)

where the payoff of $Q''$ is given by $\pi''$ where:

$$\pi'' = \frac{2M - 8V}{25M} \left( (V + M) + \sqrt{(V + M)^2 - 5VM} \right) - \frac{V}{5}$$
(3)

where in a competitive market the given payoff balances security enhancement against engineering overhead.

3. Moving-Target Defense & Equilibria

Integrating diminishing returns into a functional model of security implementation is ideal, since overcompensating for security weakness by excessive investment does not yield the same level of security enhancement at an instantaneous point over time [19]. Factoring other variables within development costs requires expansion of the dimensions of the Nash Equilibrium, where each budgetary decision has an impact on vulnerability to attackers:

$$\frac{\partial \pi''}{\partial x_c} = 0 \implies x_c^* = \text{Optimal Defense Allocation}$$
(4)

Moving-target defense (MTD) techniques [9], [10] introduce spatial dynamism by rotating configuration states, cryptographic salts, and port bindings. When integrated with Stackelberg leader-follower dynamics [11], the defender moves first by establishing fortification boundaries, forcing the adversary to commit finite exploit budgets against sub-optimal vectors.

4. Conclusions

We anticipate a large possible field of study where managerial and resource allocation decisions can be adaptable within a game theory framework using different model styles and techniques. Further research may be conducted specifically to study the game theoretical implications of applying these models in high-tech and digital industries where most pre-product development resources are expended on software engineers and security fortification.
