You Can See Everything

Thomas Koetting
→ Original Review Post

Having just finished watching You Can See Everything with Clara and Paul, I find myself reaffirming my personal rule against assigning numerical ratings to films and art in general—especially after an experience like this. While my gut feeling is to give it five stars, assigning a rating to it feels reductive; it fails to do the work justice. But I do feel like it would be interesting to share my thoughts with you all!

For the majority of its runtime, I found that the film was forcing me to confront the reality that absolute truth always evades our grasp. The universe is unfathomably complex, and human beings naturally construct narratives and simplifications just to cope with the fact that we can never grasp the true depth of what has occurred.

Watching Elizabeth Holmes, I kept asking myself the same question: Why does she think the way she does? Is she neurodivergent? Is this the downstream effect of trauma from her past? It feels wrong to definitively call someone “not normal” or to define the way which they act as requiring an explanation that relies on something outside of a baseline state.

Looking at her made me reflect on myself and the people in my own life. So many of us go through the world pretending we understand reality, when in truth, none of us do. Worse still, we pretend to understand ourselves. When really, our memories and inner mental models of ourselves are malleable, and are constantly shaped by forces outside our conscious awareness. When someone holds a core belief with enough intensity—particularly the belief that they are fundamentally good, worthy, or right—the mind will perform incredible mental gymnastics to maintain that feeling of internal consistency. It will warp memories, selectively edit history, and invent rationalizations for various things, whether consciously or subconsciously. It’s like having someone who retroactively alters Wikipedia pages to form a historical narrative they wish to be true, while also simultaneously not being fully conscious they are doing so, so they believe their own altered story.

This complexity was displayed in Elizabeth and Billy’s relationship. Even if their dynamic appeared very “off” or toxic, their love for each other (though Billy’s motivation for his affection seems to be questionable) would appear to be self-evident. The justifications, emotional compromises, and internal logic sustaining that relationship are so convoluted that I cannot even begin to dissect them. To do so would require the ability to fully understand myself and the contradictions and misinformation I tell myself, which is totally impossible. I would have to account for every lie I have told myself, every uncomfortable fact I ignore, and every fabricated memory I have. None of us can do that with complete clarity without letting the bias that created these illusions in the first place alter our perception of them.

Walking out of the theater, I spoke to Clara and Paul about feeling a sense of frustrating near-comprehension during the scenes where Elizabeth tried to explain why people accused her of fraud, stating her firm belief that Theranos’ technology worked even while she simultaneously failed to articulate why it ultimately didn’t. Looking into Elizabeth’s eyes on screen, I kept seeing the faint, blurry outline of motives and connections, the subconscious architecture driving her choices. Yet every time I reached out to grasp that to truly understand why, it was just out of reach. It reminded me of the myth of how Tantalus was punished.

In that sense, I appreciate that the film refuses a tidy resolution. It resists the lazy narrative of painting Billy as a pure evil manipulator and Elizabeth as a naive victim preyed upon by an entrepreneurial schemer. The contrast between Amanda Seyfried’s interpretation of Elizabeth’s motives in the final meeting’s transcript versus Nathan’s really speaks to me about just how much our perception depends on the way we wish things to be and not how they actually are.

When I watch Elizabeth Holmes, the motives I project onto her are just that: my own subjective interpretations of her actions. This subjectivity mirrors how we approach other existential concepts. When someone says they believe in God or that God spoke directly to them, how can that ever be empirically proven or disproven? If absolute truth could be definitively found and displayed, I think many of us gather around it instantly.

Yet pure, unadulterated truth remains the one thing humanity cannot reach—no matter how sincere we might try to be. No matter how big the particle accelerators we construct are, our nature prevents us from reaching it. We are finite and emotional creatures trying to view an almost limitless concept from a single handicapped perspective.

This realization challenged my own reflexive assumptions. Prior to this film, I had comfortably categorized Elizabeth Holmes as a “bad person”, filing her alongside figures like Bernie Madoff or Kenneth Lay. Watching the documentary didn’t convince me she is innocent—I still believe she committed fraud—but it made me realize how little I actually know about her internal reality. We treat our moral intuitions as gospel, forgetting how heavily they rely on our personal wiring and social conditioning.

I think Hannah Arendt’s idea of the banality of evil is something I find incredibly applicable to a case like Elizabeth Holmes. That, “evil”, as commonly defined, is often carried out by shallow individuals driven by “careerism, self-deception, ideological clichés”, and an inability to view things from the perspective of those they harm. We see this pattern of behavior a lot as a result of America’s “self-made man” ethos, strongly prioritizing individual success and performative success.

One of my favorite series, Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood, revolves around the realization that objective truth is beyond humanity’s grasp, and true philosophical maturity lies in acceptance of our finite nature and living within those limits. Having previously been religious and now not, I am always struck by how people can be so certain of the authoritative truth of things/concepts that cannot be proven.

This reminds me of Immanuel Kant’s distinction between noumena, reality independent of human perception of things-in-themselves, and phenomena, the perception of reality which we construct within our minds. He posits that, like Truth outlines in FMAB, full unbridled noumena is totally unknowable, and we only interact with appearances. As to what it is in order to accede to personal ideas of what the nature concepts in the Noumena are (God, the nature of reality, etc.) postulates of practical reason. Hiromu Arakawa drew more from Plato’s Realm of Forms when writing Truth in Fullmetal Alchemist, she still comes to the Kantian conclusion that human beings cannot move outside their own cognitive limits to view a total objective truth.

My takeaway is not that we should surrender to cynical nihilism and declare that nothing matters or that truth doesn’t exist. But instead we should:

“Walk the Earth on two feet as I do. Find value in humility. Then, you will find peace.”

People, like the universe itself, are unfathomably layered. When we look at someone and instantly label them a hero or a villain, we are mistaking our projection for the actual truth.

In his writings on Absurdism, Albert Camus observed the core contradiction of the human condition, our profound yearning for meaning, purpose, and justice collides with the indifference of a silent universe that offers none (this is a broad ontological claim about truth that I cannot claim to actually know for certain since I just spent multiple paragraphs explaining why I align with epistemic skepticism). Rather than sitting with that ambiguity, we project moral simplicity onto reality. We divide people into binaries: honest versus liar, trustworthy versus fraud. Elizabeth Holmes lied; therefore, she is simply a liar.

Watching You Can See Everything served as an important reminder to me of our shared, messy humanity. Elizabeth Holmes may not be a cartoon villain; she is a deeply flawed, struggling human being who made wrong and self-deceptive decisions. That distinction does not excuse or reduce the real-world harm she caused, but in an era dominated by political and moral polarization, leaving room for nuance is very important in my view. We must recognize our universal blind spots we all have before we judge the blind spots of others.

I think writing this has made me reflect on my contradicting beliefs in both subjective phenomenology and epistemic fallibilism, where the former posits we can only know our conscious experience is certain to exist, while the latter describes the ability for us to discern partial knowledge of an objective truth that exists outside of ourselves. I guess I live my life in a way that espouses fallibilism while when I really get down to it, subjective phenomenology seems to be coherent yet nihilistic perspective that I don’t often think about. Descartes’ iconic “I think, therefore I am” and the nature of the ability to only definitively know that you exist does strike a chord with me though, but I’m just rambling now.
