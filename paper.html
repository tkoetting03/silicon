Can Non-Cooperative Game Theory Assist Modeling Security Implementation for Back-End Web App Development?

T. Koetting
E–mail: tkoettin@stumail.jccc.edu
↓ Download Original PDF Document (5 Pages)

Abstract — In active development environments of web-based applications, back-end engineers and developers are constantly doing battle with bad actors probing for exploits. We explore the potential of non-cooperative game theory as a framework for enhancing back-end web development security and resource management. Most traditional strategies implemented by companies focus on static defense and reactive methods. By adopting a perspective influenced by game theory, we delve into the back and forth nature of the development timeline. We assume that the two exclusive contributors to the game in this framework are the development team and the bad actors. We finally explore whether or not the model being implemented should have characteristics aligned with flexibility or rigidity, and what influence their implementation environment has on the model’s final structure.

Key words — Game Theory – Web-App Development – Development Models

1. Introduction

Game theory has found myriad uses in security and infrastructure planning within organizations. Many aspects of non-cooperative game theory have been explored in network and IT with the two prominent model categories being security attack-defense analysis and security measurement [1]. For most purposes within security, most of these models are decidedly competitive and therefore non-cooperative due to the fact that both attackers and defenders are working to maximize their own payoff at the expense of their adversary [13].

There is also the given assumption depending upon the model used of known information, with the most realistic and computationally expensive models being Bayesian Games, which fall under the category of games of incomplete information, but Bayesian games also allow for knowledge of the payoffs of the adversary on both sides of the game, a situation which parallels active security efforts and offensive plans by the defending networks and attackers alike [14].

Through an evaluation function the central defender can make decisions about which nodes and plans of action to take in terms of defensive strategy. Within a network architecture, there is a need for multiple end point defense with moving attacks targeting different network nodes requiring distributed responses and strategies that are capable of choosing the most formidable defense while expending the least resources.

These scenarios are also highly applicable to the exploitation prevention measures taken by defending web applicable developers; however, security research has sparsely touched game theory’s applications to software level environments and situations [2], with the bulk of the research focusing on active threats within IT infrastructure and the active reactions taken by the defending player and by the attacker [3]. The planning of infrastructure, whether classical or IT infrastructure, can be shown to have some applicability to development, in the sense that given a significant element of software where exploits or vulnerabilities may exist given inability to completely close off offensive opportunities due to budget constraints, where, for example given [3] and reinforced power lines, developers must make strategic allocations.

2. Management Science & Software Functionality

Extensive work has also been done in the field dealing with software business competition, licensing dynamics [4], and development expenditures in relation to business decisions, with its main focus trending towards management science rather than security [5, 7]. The most applicable model within the management frameworks is that proposed by Hui & Tam [4] on game theoretical models applying to software functionality considerations in monopoly and duopoly markets, where higher software functionality has decreasing return and greater strain depending on the development team size and budget [18].

When given the software functionality $Q$ and its second-order derivative $Q''$:

$$Q'' = \frac{2}{5M} \left( (V + M) + \sqrt{(V + M)^2 - 5VM} \right) \tag{2}$$

where the payoff of $Q''$ is given by $\pi''$:

$$\pi'' = \frac{2M - 8V}{25M} \left( (V + M) + \sqrt{(V + M)^2 - 5VM} \right) - \frac{V}{5} \tag{3}$$

where in a competitive market the given payoff balances security enhancement against engineering overhead. Integrating diminishing returns of the respective model into a functional model of security implementation is ideal, since overcompensating for security weakness by excessive investment does not yield the same level of security enhancement at an instantaneous point over time [19].

3. Moving-Target Defense & Equilibria

The factoring of the other variables within development costs also requires expansion of the dimensions of the Nash Equilibrium, where each budgetary decision has an impact on the corresponding level of investment and vulnerability.

In a dynamic game model with infinite stages $T$, the attacker's and defender's mixed strategies are formulated as:

$$(\delta_i, 1 - \delta_i) = \sum_{T=1}^{\infty} \sum_{i} \left( \frac{P_a - C_a^T - C_w^T}{P_a - C_i^T + U_a}, \, \frac{U_a - C_w^T}{P_a - C_i^T + U_a} \right) \tag{8}$$
$$(\sigma_i, 1 - \sigma_i) = \sum_{T=1}^{\infty} \sum_{i} \left( \frac{C_m^T}{C_i^T}, \, \frac{C_i^T - C_m^T}{C_i^T} \right) \tag{9}$$

The corresponding payoff of the game model is:

$$U_{\text{game}} = \sum_{T=1}^{\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{\lambda} U_I = \sum_{T=1}^{\infty} \sum_{i=1}^{\lambda} \left( C_i^T \theta_i \sigma_i + U_i - C_m^T \theta_i - C_i^T \sigma_i \right) \tag{10}$$

4. Discussion & Conclusion

Given the set algorithm and pattern of the strategies implemented by the defenders or attackers, and the thus predictable payoffs, we can conclude that while useful in their ability to be implemented into moving target defense algorithms and predictable resource allocation schedules, non-cooperative game theory provides rigorous bounds for decision makers navigating finite security budgets.
